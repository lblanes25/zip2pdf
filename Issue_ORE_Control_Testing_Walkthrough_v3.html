The question

If this activity failed on its own — everything else done correctly — would the risk actually materialize?

Yes → it is its own control. No → it is an attribute of the control it supports.

Why the obvious test doesn’t work: people reach for “different person, different system, different evidence”. A monthly reconciliation has a preparer and a reviewer, separate people and separate evidence — and nobody splits it into two controls. Those differences tell you to ask the question. They don’t answer it.

Rule 1 · Failure logic

If the activity can fail on its own — with everything else working — and cause the risk outcome, treat it as a separate control.

Rule 2 · Ownership

A control needs one owner. If activities cross a function boundary, split the control even where the activities support the same risk response.

Important: different people, systems or evidence are clues to investigate the boundary. They do not determine it by themselves.

Same timing does not mean the same control. The question is not whether two activities happen at the same moment. It is whether they are one integrated act, or whether one can still fail after the other has succeeded.

What the RCM row looks like before and after the walkthrough

The RCM should exist during planning so leadership can see what the team intends to test. The walkthrough does not create the test plan from scratch; it validates and refines a planning hypothesis. The row becomes more specific as facts are confirmed, and may split into multiple rows if the walkthrough establishes separate controls. Building it before the walkthrough is the right sequence — it just has to be built as something that will change.

A process walkthrough and a control walkthrough answer different questions. Process understanding is written to explain how work flows end to end. Control boundaries need a narrower set of facts — whether one activity can fail after another has succeeded, and who owns each one. A process walkthrough can be complete and still not answer those, so expect to go back for specific facts. That is a normal second pass, not rework.

Before the test can be executed

Once the control boundary is clear, the next question is whether each attribute is specific enough to test. The walkthrough closes the facts the current record does not tell us.

CriteriaWhich enterprise requirements apply, and which version?
TimingWhat is the required timeline for recording?
CompletenessWhich fields or information are required?
ReviewWho is authorized to perform review and challenge, and when?
EvidenceWhat demonstrates the assessment, review conclusion and system entry?
Where the process walkthrough livesFor the pilot: a separate Word document attached to the audit, referenced from Section 3 of the workpaper. Use the draft template or your own — it is not vetted yet, and the workpaper only needs the reference.
CoverageHow does G&C know every identified Gap was referred? If nothing detects omissions, this may be a Process Understanding coverage gap rather than an attribute.
What I need from you. Walk me from a Gap arriving to the record existing — how many systems does it touch? Who performs the review and challenge? What are the “applicable enterprise requirements” — which policy, which version? What is the required timeline for recording? And how does G&C know it has received every Gap the business identified?

The first two decide the structure. The next two decide whether the attributes can be tested at all. The last one may not be a control question — if nothing detects unreferred Gaps, that is a coverage gap for the Process Understanding workstep.
